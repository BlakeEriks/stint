Home

The screen opened fifty times a day. It answers what a contractor cannot answer from memory: how much money is sitting unbilled, whether the month is on pace, what the last quarter actually grossed, and where the work went.

The frame — header, rail, dock, timer bar — is _shell.html. This page specifies the content column, which is the panel.

What earns a place

Toggl's dashboard answers how did I spend my time? For a solo contractor that is the wrong question — you already know, you were there.

  • A region ships only if it carries a number the user cannot compute in their head, or a row they can click to act on. Preferably both. "Interesting" is not the bar.
  • Stats are denominated in money. Hours are the raw material; dollars are what the user thinks in. Unbillable work has no rate by definition, so those rows show hours and an em-dash.
  • GET /stats is one call. The regions render together, and a set that pops in piecemeal reads as broken.
  • Nothing here writes. Every region reads, and every action on one is a link to the surface that owns the mutation — a dashboard that edits data turns a stray click into a changed invoice.
  • There is one view. No hiding a region, no reordering them. "Pick your own dashboard" is the most natural path by which a restraint-first product becomes the thing it was built against.
One surface, three rows

The content column is a single borderless panel on the ground. The regions are parts of it, not cards on it: nothing inside the panel carries a border, a background or a shadow of its own. Rows of regions separate by an inset rule with equal space above and below.

The panel is wide enough for two columns, and the regions pair by subject: a figure beside the breakdown that explains it. Unbilled sits beside who owes it; the month sits beside the quarter; where the time went sits beside when it was spent.

Three rows. The rules are inset and horizontal; nothing inside has an edge of its own
  • Row order is fixed: money waiting, then whether the month is working, then where the work went. No hiding a region, no reordering them.
  • The columns size by @container, not viewport. The panel is not the window: the rail and the dock claim their space at lg and xl, so the panel is 780px at a 1100px window and 732px at 1440 — wider at the narrower window. A viewport breakpoint would collapse the wide one and split the narrow one.
  • Below the threshold the pairs stack, in the order they read: Unbilled, By client, the month, Gross earned, By project, the half-year.
  • The left column is the wider, at 1.15fr to 1fr. It carries the figures, and an equal split leaves client names truncating while the money column has room to spare.
  • Every row takes that same ratio, so one gutter runs the height of the panel. A row on its own split puts the gutter somewhere else; one that lands on the other side of the panel's centre from the row above reads as a mistake rather than as an asymmetry anyone chose.
  • Unbilled hides itself with nothing outstanding, and By-client goes with it. A region with no rows is a heading over a gap.
  • Rows size by @container too. The same row renders in this panel and in the narrow dock, where a sm: breakpoint is true at 1600px and lays out as though there were room.

Separation is space and an inset horizontal rule, nothing else.

  • Never a vertical rule between the columns. It rebuilds the gridlines this panel exists without, and reads the pair back as two cards.
  • Never a full-bleed horizontal rule. Inset to the rows' own 16px; edge to edge it cuts the panel in two and reads as two stacked cards.
The region header, in two modes

Every title on this screen is a quiet label, because every region's point is the thing under it. A figure region sets that figure beneath the label; a picture region gives the label the line and puts its one control opposite. The label is what makes six regions in one panel scan as six parts of one screen rather than six headings competing.

Unbilled

The headline number, and the reason this screen exists. Work done and not yet invoiced — money the user might still never see. The figure is the left half of the first row; By client is the right.

  • Never labelled "earned" or "revenue". Overstating it in a billing tool is the same trust failure as silently editing an entry.
  • By-client is its own region, beside the figure. Paired, the total and its breakdown are read together; stacked, they push every other region a screenful down.
  • It takes a quiet label, not a heading. It is the second half of one subject, and a heading at region weight announces it as a third.
  • Each row links to invoice generation for that client, which is what makes the region an action rather than a readout.
  • Work with no resolvable rate shows an em-dash, not $0.00. A zero looks like a real figure and understates the total; unratedCount says the total is incomplete rather than low.
  • Awaiting payment is never added to the total. That is work not yet invoiced; this is money already asked for, and summing them double-counts the same hours.
  • Past five clients the remainder is a count, the same shape the half-year's legend uses.
The month

A cumulative line of accumulated hours — or money, following the goal's unit — against the goal ray. The gap between the two lines is the delta, rendered as a distance rather than a percentage to decode.

No target — the region stays, carrying the way in
  • The ray steps on business days only. A ray sloping through the weekend shows the user behind every Saturday and recovered every Monday, which is the noise the business-day rule exists to kill.
  • The actual line stops at today and is null beyond it. A line drawn flat to the 31st reads as a month that stopped working, not a month still in progress.
  • Weekend work is carried onto the next business day's point, never discarded, so the cumulative total always equals the month's actual.
  • The target is context, not part of the figure — set at row scale beside it rather than carried along at 30px.
  • With no target the region stays, carrying a line that names what a goal is for and a link to the field. Hiding it made the feature invisible to the only account that had never set one.
  • The scale is the target, unless the month ran past it — a line that leaves the top of the box would misreport a month that beat its goal.
  • Settings owns the goal field. The pencil is a link, never a dialog: a second editor here is a second place to look when the number is wrong.
Velocity

Gross over the trailing three months, per month, and the mix that made it up. It sits beside the month plot, and it is a different object from Unbilled: a figure, a bar, and a key.

  • The mix is a bar, never a row list. Unbilled is a figure over per-client rows. A second region in that shape — same columns, same trailing arrow, and with a full book the same order of magnitude — is read at a glance as the first one printed twice.
  • The bar is the region's picture, one segment per client, sized by that client's share of the gross.
  • The segments are muted. At full strength the client hues across the panel's width pull harder than the running timer, which is the one thing on screen allowed to shout.
  • The key is inline text with a swatch — a name and a figure. No aging column, no amount column, no arrow: those are the row shape this region exists not to be.
  • The figure is per month, with /mo gross beside it. The window total carries no rate, so two windows of different lengths cannot be compared by it.
  • "Gross earned", never "earned" alone. It is work done over the window, not money collected, and /mo gross is what says so.
  • The invoiced/unbilled split is not printed. Its figures are window totals while the headline is per month, so they do not reconcile with the number above them, and the unbilled half restates what the Unbilled region already says. The gap still reads when it matters: Unbilled's own total exceeding this window's gross is the signal.
  • The headline takes the paid beat, alone. A payment moves money across the split without changing the gross, so the figure does not move — the colour marks the event, and it leaves with the tween.
  • Not comparable with awaiting payment, which spans every period rather than this window.
  • A client's share is its gross, invoiced plus unbilled. A client whose whole window is still unbilled grosses its unbilled amount, not nothing.

  • The name owns its own line, above the bar. Project names run past twenty characters, and this half gives a label under a column about 92px — every one of them truncates. Across the region's full width none of them does.
  • A bar's length is its share of the longest bar, not of the window's total. The question is which project is biggest and by how much; shares of the sum flatten four near-equal projects into four quarter-width stubs.
  • The client is not drawn, and the hue carries it. Naming it costs the project name the line it needs, which is the trade this shape exists to make.
  • The fill is 6px at the mix's own strength, the weight Velocity's bar uses two rows above: the screen's two pictures made of a fill read as one thing at one strength.
  • The label sits above the fill, never on it. Text crossing a fill's end changes contrast mid-word.
  • A project with no client takes the internal swatch, the neutral that reads as worked.
  • Four bars, and the rest is a count. The region does not scroll — the tail of a solo book is projects worth an hour each, and the full ranking belongs to a screen built to rank.
  • With one project it draws tasks, and the heading says so — By task · Betterlife. One client and one project is where most books start, and a single full-length bar is a chart that failed. The fall-through is a function of the data: no click, no selection, nothing remembered. Tasks group on a trimmed, case-folded task_name and display the most recent spelling, because the column is free text and two spellings of one task would otherwise be two bars.
  • The toggle switches the unit, never the window. Hours and revenue reorder the same four projects; a window control here would be a second range on a screen whose ranges are all fixed.
  • Unbillable work has hours and no revenue. On revenue it drops out of the ranking rather than sitting at zero, and the count line is where it goes.
  • The footer line carries the window and the remainder, right-aligned. The logged total is the one figure here that is not a bar. The window is abbreviated — 3mo — because Velocity names it in full directly above and the long form wraps at this width.
The half-year

Twenty-six weeks, one cell per day. Colour is the client, density is the hours. Half a year is long enough to show an engagement beginning and ending, and a quiet stretch as a stretch rather than a bad week.

  • Hue is the client, resolved as everywhere else, and never the accent — that belongs to the running timer. Internal work keeps a neutral that reads as worked rather than as rest.
  • A blank day stays blank. Every day in the window gets a cell, so a gap is a weekend or a dry spell rather than missing data.
  • A day split across clients takes the one with the most hours. A cell is about 12px and cannot carry a stack; the alternative is a smear of colour that names nobody.
  • Archived clients keep their colour. Work billed to a finished engagement belongs in the history, and dropping the hue would silently reassign those hours to the neutral band.
  • The busiest day sets the density scale, floored so the lightest worked day is still visibly a day worked.
  • The streak sits in the header at row scale. It survives one missed day and breaks on two — a streak that breaks on a single missed day punishes one appointment and stops being a figure anyone trusts. Today not yet worked does not break it.
  • Each cell's date and hours are in its tooltip. There is no axis; at 26 columns every label is noise.
The beats

Three moments where a figure moves rather than replaces itself. The screen's largest numbers change for reasons the user cannot compute in their head — a four-level rate lookup, an invoice clearing — and landing on the new figure without the travel makes the old one look wrong.

  • On stop, Unbilled counts to its new value, with the day's earnings beside it. Both are the resolved amount, which is the number the user cannot arrive at alone.
  • That chip is today's running total, not the last stop. Three stops at $40, $30 and $80 read +$150 today. It survives a refresh and resets at local midnight, because it is a fact about the day rather than a flash about a fetch.
  • Raising an invoice never subtracts from it. The money leaves Unbilled without any work being undone, and a day's earnings that fall when the user invoices them is the screen disagreeing with what happened.
  • The stop beat is neutral, never green. The accent is the running timer, and a stop has just ended one — borrowing it marks as live the one thing that stopped being live.
  • An unbillable stop moves the hours and says so. It resolves to no money, and a stop that moved nothing would teach the user that marking work billable is what makes the app react: the UI arguing with the data's honesty. It reports what happened rather than praising it.
  • On marking an invoice paid, Unbilled counts down while Velocity's headline flashes, together. Without the pairing the screen's largest number shrinks at the moment the user got paid, which reads as a loss. The gross itself does not move — the work was already done — so here the colour alone carries the event.
  • That beat is the one legitimate use of the success green here, and it leaves with the tween. It is the only outcome on the screen; nothing else earns the colour.
  • On arrival, the figure animates from what this browser last displayed. The line beneath the day names a different period: it measures against a baseline taken once per local day, so it says the same thing however often the app is opened.
  • Last-seen is per-device, in localStorage under one key per origin — a display detail, never account state. Two devices disagreeing is correct: each animates what it has not shown you.
  • A first load never animates. With nothing stored there is no period to name, and counting up from zero would report the user's entire history as though it had just happened. No stored value renders settled, then stores.
  • Reduced motion renders the settled figure — not a jump to zero and not a skipped render. The number is the content, so suppressing the animation must leave the answer on screen.
  • An animation never leaves a figure showing anything but the server's value. The tween ends on the target itself, never on its last interpolation: a billing screen that settles a cent off has silently changed what it reports.
  • The regions still never mutate. The beats are reactions to mutations owned by other screens, read off /stats — which every one of them already invalidates.
Constraints this screen inherits
  • Nothing here is green. The accent is spent on the running timer in the bar below.
  • Warning is the only alert channel, and the month's delta is the only thing on this screen that uses it. The success green is spent on the paid beat and nowhere else — it is the only outcome the screen has.
  • Durations and currency are mono and tabular, without exception. An unbilled column that reflows as digits change is the same defect as a timer that does.
  • Aggregate rate resolution belongs in SQL. resolve_entry_rate(uuid) is per-entry; Unbilled and Velocity sum across hundreds of entries and must not call it N times. A set-returning rollup is the correct shape.
  • 0 is a valid rate. Null-coalescing, never truthiness.
Rejected — each will look appealing again
  • Hiding or reordering regions. There is one view. A dashboard the user assembles is a dashboard nobody can specify, and it is the path by which this becomes the product it was built against.
  • A range control on the heatmap. The window is fixed at 26 weeks. A picker turns one honest shape into four that disagree, and each switch refetches the lot.
  • Matching the heatmap to the row's trailing quarter. Velocity and By project both report three months, so one window for the whole row is tempting. Thirteen weeks in this column is a 26.7px cell and a block nearly as tall as the chart beside it — a calendar rather than a texture — and capping the cell leaves a third of the column empty. The window is also the region's whole reason to exist: at a quarter it shows what the two regions beside it already do.
  • Stacking a split day's clients in one cell. At 11px a stack is a smear. The majority takes the cell.
  • Project shades in the heatmap. The cell already spends lightness on hours, and a shade on the same channel inverts rather than blurs: on a four-step scale at ΔL 0.080, the lightest step at 35% opacity composites to L 0.437 while the darkest at full strength reaches L 0.540, so the lightest project on a quiet day renders darker than the darkest on a busy one. At 35% the steps are ΔL 0.032, under half what they were drawn at. A day takes its client's base hue.
  • Clicking a project to expand its tasks. Which project is selected is per-user view state, which is the assembled dashboard under another name, and it leaves the region with no fixed subject to pair against. The one-project case is served by falling through to tasks on the data alone.
  • A ranked project table. By client is text rows — an aging column, an amount column, a trailing arrow into invoice generation. Giving projects the same columns builds a second table that acts like the first one but goes nowhere, since there is no per-project screen to arrive at. The bar is the answer here: a length against the longest is a proportion read without comparing two columns of digits.
  • Pie charts. A solo contractor's time splits two to four ways. A bar does that in less space and stays readable when one slice is 4%.
  • Team and comparison stats. There is no team. This is the slot Toggl wastes.
What this screen takes from the API
CallFeedsNote
GET /stats Unbilled, the month, Velocity, By project Unbilled by client with aging, the month's cumulative series against its ray, trailing-quarter gross by client, and the billable ratio. One request, because they render together. The same window by project, in both units, is not implemented — it is a SQL aggregate through resolve_rate() and a byProject shape in @stint/schema (tasks.md).
GET /calendar?granularity=day the half-year One call over a 26-week range. Already groups by local day server-side, which is the DST-correct bucketing this needs and the reason not to build a second one. Returns { date, totalSeconds, byClient } and nothing else.
GET /clients?includeArchived the half-year's hues and legend Archived clients included, so a finished engagement keeps its colour instead of falling into the neutral band.

The half-year's cache key is its own, not a variant of the activity key: the range is fixed, so sharing one with a range the user picks would refetch 182 days on every change. It joins invalidateEntryData(), or the region goes stale on every timer stop.

The target lives on user_settings as monthly_target and monthly_target_unit, paired by a check constraint so neither can be set without the other — so the Settings field writes both in one patch, and clearing one clears both. Revenue's figure comes from month_revenue(), whose coalesce chain must stay identical to the other three — see data-model.md.
